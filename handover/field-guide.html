01Why it exists

A wardrobe leaves a Cityfurnish warehouse. Four different systems are supposed to notice: the security guard writes it in the gate register, the warehouse team logs it in an ops spreadsheet, the delivery agent scans it in the Delivery Tracker app, and someone posts the stock move in Odoo.

Each is kept by different people, for different reasons, at different moments. So they drift apart. And when they drift, nobody knows which one is wrong — or whether a unit is genuinely missing.

Before this system, that reconciliation happened on WhatsApp every morning: someone eyeballing a sheet against Odoo, chasing what looked odd. It caught the obvious and missed the rest, and it never produced a number anyone could trust.

Auto-Reco replaces that. Every night it compares all four records unit by unit — matched on the barcode, not on quantities — and asks a single question of each one: which books have this item, and which don't?

The thing to hold onto

The system does not count stock. It compares records. Its output is never "you are short 14 wardrobes" — it is "these specific units are recorded in some places and not others, and here is who needs to fix each one."

02The four books, and why they are not equal

Throughout the product you will see these four named. Learn them — almost everything else follows from how they differ.

BookWho keeps itWhat it proves
GateSecurity guard at the gateA human physically saw the unit cross the threshold
SheetWarehouse ops teamWhat we intended, and whether it actually completed
TrackerDelivery agent, in the DT appThe field scan at the customer's door
OdooWhoever posts the stock moveThe book entry — what finance and inventory see

Only one of them knows whether the delivery worked

This is the single most important asymmetry in the system. Only the ops sheet records an outcome. The other three are filtered to completed records before we ever read them, so they all say "done" by definition. A gate entry saying "done" means the unit crossed the gate — not that the customer received it.

So a failed delivery looks like this: the sheet says Not Delivered, and the other three are silent. That silence is correct, not a discrepancy — and the system has to know the difference, or every failed delivery becomes a false alarm.

Only two of them carry a clock

Odoo and the Tracker record real timestamps. The gate register and the ops sheet carry a date somebody wrote by hand, with no time at all. That single fact shapes the whole design of the day, which is the next section.

03The daily rhythm

A business day here runs 3:00 pm to 3:00 pm, not midnight to midnight. That is not an arbitrary choice — it is when the gate register is physically ruled off and handed over. The system's day matches the warehouse's day.

So "Tuesday" means Tuesday 3pm through Wednesday 3pm.

3:00 pmThe day closesThe register is ruled off and handed to the office.
before 8pmOps must have filedRegister uploaded, ops sheet filled in. Miss this and the night's comparison runs without you — and the email names your city.
8:00 pmThe comparison runsAll four records are pulled and matched unit by unit.
9:00 pmThe email goes outA digest to founders and managers: accuracy per city, what's open, the biggest gap.

Two details that trip people up:

  • We deliberately run two days behind. Tuesday's business day is reconciled on Thursday evening, not Wednesday. Odoo postings routinely land a day late, so holding one day back stops us raising alarms about entries that were about to arrive anyway.
  • 8pm was chosen with data, not a guess. The run used to be at 4:30pm. Only 8 registers out of 38 had ever arrived by then; by 8pm it was 15. Moving the run was cheaper than nagging five warehouses.

And a consequence worth knowing before someone reports it as a bug: because the sheet and register carry a typed date while Odoo carries a real timestamp, a movement that happened in the morning may sit on one side of the 3pm line in one book and the other side in another. The engine allows for it. The two columns in the email will not tie out exactly, and that is expected.

04What comes out: chase it, or note it

Every disagreement the system finds gets a name — there are 23 of them — and every name sits in one of two buckets. This is the distinction the whole product turns on.

  • Chase — a genuine gap. Somebody has to do something today. This is the number in the email subject line. There are 11 of these.
  • Note — data hygiene. A late posting, a mis-typed barcode, a missing scan where the other three books agree. Kept for the record, deliberately excluded from the loss count. There are 12.

A manager works this list on the dashboard: search it, filter it, and close items with a reason — late entry, data-entry error, transit delay, theft, and so on. Closing requires a reason, always. That is what turns the queue into something you can analyse later rather than just empty.

05The rules that stop false alarms

A reconciliation system that cries wolf gets ignored within a fortnight, and then it may as well not exist. Most of the engineering here is not about finding discrepancies — that part is easy — it is about refusing to raise ones that aren't real.

A book that didn't report is never blamed

If Odoo is unreachable one night, every unit will look "missing from Odoo." Without a guard against this, one outage produces hundreds of urgent false alarms and destroys trust in the tool overnight. So each rule checks that a book actually filed before it will blame it for an absence. A zero is not the same as silence, and the system stores both separately.

Spares and packing boxes are counted, never matched

Spare parts, consumables and PP boxes live in the register, the sheet and the app — but never in Odoo, because they aren't serialised. Matched like units, they would look permanently missing. They are tallied as quantities instead and kept out of the chase list entirely.

Closed warehouses are expected to be silent

Mumbai, Hyderabad and Pune close on Thursdays, and there are one-off holidays too. On those days, empty records mean the warehouse was shut — not that data is missing. The closure calendar is read live from the delivery app's own settings, so when ops change it, this system follows without anybody deploying anything.

Late Odoo postings are not missing postings

Odoo's timestamp is when someone posted the move, not when the goods moved — and roughly half get posted the next day. Vendor receipts are worse: one measurement found all 162 purchase-inward rows posted two or three days after the goods arrived. Telling a warehouse to "post this in Odoo" when it is already posted is the single most common false alarm they report, so these are recognised and demoted to a note.

06The gate app — the newest and largest piece

Of the four books, the gate register was by far the weakest. It was handwritten, then scanned, then read by character-recognition software. Two measurements settled it: the register was missing entirely for 79% of units, and of the lines that were written, the scanner recovered only 483 out of 738 on one sampled day.

So it is being replaced by a phone app the guard uses at the gate. They scan the QR code on the item sticker; a scan either returns the exact serial or fails, and never returns a wrong one — which a human copying digits by hand cannot promise.

The design decision worth understanding

The guard is never shown what is expected

The app knows perfectly well which items should be on the truck. It never tells the guard. Show someone the list and they will scan against the list — the record stops being what a person saw and becomes a confirmation of what Odoo already believed. Four independent books quietly collapse into three, and the one meant to catch the others now agrees with them by construction.

The check still runs, and the result is still recorded. A manager reads it afterwards in the portal, which is exactly where a discrepancy belongs. This has been re-argued twice and written into the code as a constant so the reasoning cannot be lost.

How it is built for a gate at night

  • Offline first. Every action saves to the phone and syncs later. A guard never waits on a network request with a truck idling.
  • The phone is the least trusted thing in the system. It proposes; the server decides the date, city, location check and everything else. Re-sending is harmless — double-counting an outward movement would invent stock leaving the building.
  • Attendance runs on the device. The check-in photo is matched against a reference stored on the phone; only the result is uploaded. No face image goes to a third party — the cleanest position under India's data protection law, and it works with no signal.
  • Location is a flag, never a refusal. Gates are pinned to a 200m radius. A guard outside it still records the movement; it is marked for a manager to look at. "We couldn't tell" is recorded as unknown, never as a failure.
  • Five languages — English, Hindi, Marathi, Kannada, Telugu.

08Where it stands, August 2026

Live and running nightly across all five cities. Thirty-five database changes deployed, and 789 automated checks that must pass before anything ships.

  • Just fixedThe portal no longer claims a clean bill of health when it failed to checkWhen data couldn't be fetched, screens displayed "Everything is accounted for" — a stocktaker who couldn't get into the warehouse writing "all present and correct". Now every screen distinguishes an empty result from a failed one, and the summary figures show a dash rather than a confident zero.
  • OpenMumbai sits at 32.9% accuracy338 items where only the ops sheet has any record. It predates all current work and is the largest single signal in the data.
  • OpenSmall usability gapsDark mode doesn't survive a reload. The guard app's first screen offers no way to change language. Two screens leave a guard with nothing to tap. The dashboard heading still uses internal jargon we've banned everywhere else.
  • OpenDocumentation is behind the productThe written notes describe 8 database changes when there are 35, quote outdated timings, and stop before the entire gate app was built. Do not trust them over this page or the code.

09Two things waiting on a business decision

Raised three times · still unanswered

The Thursday problem. Mumbai, Hyderabad and Pune close on Thursdays. But because a business day runs 3pm to 3pm, "Thursday" now stretches into Friday morning — when those warehouses are open and working. The system still treats that whole window as closed and quietly suppresses genuine findings inside it.

If that reading is right, we are losing real results every week in three of five cities. Somebody needs to decide what the correct behaviour is; it is a question about how the business works, not a coding problem.

The second is smaller: the guard register was historically uploaded as a scanned PDF, and the gate app is replacing it city by city. There is a transition period where some cities are on the app and some are still on paper, and we should agree what the cutover looks like rather than letting it happen city by city by accident.

10If you are going to touch the code

Skip this section unless you are. It is a web application built with Next.js and TypeScript, using Supabase for the database, hosted on Vercel. It reads Odoo through Metabase, the Delivery Tracker from MongoDB, the ops sheets through the Google Sheets API, and scans registers with Azure's document reader.

Four traps that have already caused real bugs

  • Two definitions of "day" coexist. A calendar day and a 3pm business day. Change one connector's window without changing how it attributes dates and you silently reclassify half of Odoo, with no error anywhere.
  • The database silently caps a query at 1,000 rows unless you ask for a range. This once made the dashboard report 169 problems for a night that had 555.
  • Raw source data keeps every re-check pass. Filter by date alone and you get the same movement several times over — 4,106 rows for a day that pulled 896.
  • Database changes are applied by hand. Code must keep working when a migration hasn't been run yet, and degrade rather than crash the nightly job.

Two habits this codebase expects

Measure, don't assert. Every behavioural claim in the project's notes was checked against the live database. One reasonable-sounding theory about the biggest problem category turned out to explain 4 cases out of 592 — and saying so was more useful than shipping it.

Comment the why, never the what. Especially the constraint that stops the next person "fixing" something that is deliberate. Much of what looks odd in this codebase is load-bearing, and the comment explaining it is the only thing standing between you and a regression.
